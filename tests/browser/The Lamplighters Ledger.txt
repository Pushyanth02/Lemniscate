The Lamplighter's Ledger

Chapter One: The First Flame

The lamp on the landing guttered once and went out, leaving the stair to its own devices. Mrs. Vale did not look up from her needlework. "You came back," she said, as if the words cost her something.

Rain had followed him the whole way from the harbour; his coat was heavy with it, and the fire had not been lit. Nothing in the house admitted that anyone had ever left it.

He set the ledger on the table between them. It fell open to the page she knew by heart: the list of lamps he had promised to keep burning, and the dates each had gone dark.

"You kept four," she said at last. "Out of eleven."

"Four is not nothing."

"Four is a beginning," she said, and threaded her needle again.

Chapter Two: What the River Keeps

The river took the ledger in the spring, and gave back only the covers. He spent a week walking the banks, gathering pages where the mud released them, drying each one over the fire until the ink confessed what it still remembered.

Some pages had kept their numbers. Some had kept only their margins, where a younger hand had practised signing a name that was not yet his.

Mrs. Vale watched him work and said nothing, because there are kinds of grief that shrivel when they are spoken too soon.

By the eighth day he had a book again, thinner than before, and truer. He added a twelfth line to the list of lamps, and wrote beside it the name of the river.

Chapter Three: The Long Account

Years later, when the children of the lane asked what the ledger was for, he told them it was an account of debts owed to the dark: every lamp a promise that the night would not go unwitnessed.

They thought he was talking about money. He let them. The real currency of the house was attention, and it paid out nightly, in small kept fires, to anyone who learned to read the light.
